# Photosynthesis - Study Notes (Biology 101)

Photosynthesis is the process by which plants, algae and some bacteria convert light energy into chemical energy stored in glucose. The overall reaction combines carbon dioxide and water, using light, to produce glucose and oxygen.

Photosynthesis takes place inside chloroplasts, which are organelles found mainly in the leaf cells of plants. Each chloroplast contains stacks of thylakoid membranes surrounded by a fluid called the stroma.

The pigment chlorophyll absorbs mostly red and blue light and reflects green light, which is why leaves look green. Chlorophyll molecules are embedded in the thylakoid membranes where the light-dependent reactions occur.

The light-dependent reactions capture energy from sunlight to split water molecules, releasing oxygen as a by-product. These reactions produce ATP and NADPH, which are energy carriers used in the next stage.

The Calvin cycle, also called the light-independent reactions, takes place in the stroma. During the Calvin cycle the enzyme RuBisCO fixes carbon dioxide into organic molecules using the ATP and NADPH from the light reactions.

The products of the Calvin cycle are used to build glucose, which the plant uses for energy and to build cellulose for cell walls. Excess glucose can be stored as starch for later use.

The rate of photosynthesis depends on limiting factors such as light intensity, carbon dioxide concentration and temperature. Increasing light intensity raises the rate until another factor, such as carbon dioxide, becomes limiting.

Cellular respiration is essentially the reverse of photosynthesis: cells break down glucose with oxygen to release energy as ATP, producing carbon dioxide and water. Together, photosynthesis and respiration cycle carbon and oxygen through ecosystems.
